Executive Summary NomosX&#039;s position: the dominant public framing — &quot;AI regulation versus innovation&quot; — is novlangue [E91]. It conceals the two mechanisms that will actually determine the economic outcome, both of which are structural rather than substantive [E55]. The first is the discontinuity: tiered, threshold-triggered obligations generate a cliff at which firms rationally stop growing, stop reclassifying, and stop innovating [E91]. The single best-identified causal estimate available on this question is not about AI at all — it is the French administrative firm panel showing a sharp fall in the fraction of firms innovating just to the left of a size-contingent regulatory threshold, with reduced innovation responsiveness to exogenous demand shocks for firms below that threshold [E91] E91 . The AI Act is architecturally a size-and-tier-contingent regulation E83 . The mechanism ports. The second is the audit vacuum: the AI Act does not provide access to data for researchers and civil society, a regulatory gap in what would otherwise be a functioning third-party audit ecosystem [E59] E59 . A rule that cannot be independently verified does not produce trust; it produces documentation [E12]. This is the formalist trap that legal scholarship on the Brussels Effect routinely walks into — mistaking the diffusion of text for the diffusion of enforcement [E40]. The aggregate growth loss from AI regulation in Europe will be smaller than critics claim and the distributional loss larger than regulators admit [E40]. The reason is the shape of the AI-growth function: across 19 G20 countries from 2005 to 2023 using GMM with linear and quadratic terms, AI-related innovation has a positive and significant effect on economic growth, but the negative quadratic term confirms a concave relationship [E40] E40 . Concavity means the marginal social return to an additional unit of frontier AI capability is falling. If that is right, slowing the frontier by a few months costs less than is asserted — while blocking diffusion into the bottom of the firm distribution costs more [E101]. And diffusion is already the weak point: laggard firms in the bottom non établie of the productivity distribution converge toward the frontier more slowly in highly digital- and skill-intensive industries, indicating barriers to technology and knowledge diffusion [E101] E101 . Layer a threshold-contingent compliance regime on top of an already-broken diffusion channel and the result is not a slower frontier. It is a permanently thicker tail of non-adopters. The European market context sharpens the stakes rather than resolving them [E38]. The EU has lagged both quantitatively and qualitatively behind the US and China in growth over the last fifteen years while a new wave of high technology intensifies competition [E55] E55 , and EU-funded AI investment during 2014–2020 — around EUR 8 billion at NUTS2 level — went disproportionately to more developed regions, generating spillovers that reinforce existing regional specialization [E53] E53 . Regulatory burden and public investment are therefore pushing in the same direction: toward concentration [E26]. Three qualifications bound this position. First, the causal identification is borrowed, not native: E91 concerns French labor regulation at a 50-employee threshold, not AI conformity assessment, and the transfer is an analogy grounded in mechanism rather than a replication [E91]. Second, no source in this evidence base quantifies AI Act compliance cost per firm; that number is non établie, and its absence is a fact about the research, not about the regulation [E7]. Third, the counter-mechanism is real: well-specified rules can enable markets rather than suppress them, and the evidence that regulatory frameworks in the EU, US and UK currently fail to confront the challenges of generative AI [E38] E14 cuts against the assumption that the status quo ante was innovation-optimal [E8]. Implementation Roadmap Timeline: 2026-2030 Resources: Six conditions must hold. First, the AI Act&#039;s obligations must remain tier-triggered and discrete rather than being reworked into continuously scaling requirements; a continuous-obligation redesign would dissolve the threshold mechanism entirely [E91]. Second, the audit gap must persist — if the Commission legislates researcher and civil-society model access, the enforcement-vacuum branch collapses and the Brussels Effect strengthens rather than degrades [E79] E59 . Third, the concavity result must generalise beyond the G20 sample and the 2005–2023 window [E16] E40 ; if AI proves to have increasing returns at current capability levels, frontier deceleration becomes far more costly and the position inverts. Fourth, diffusion barriers in digital- and skill-intensive industries must persist [E101] E101 ; a genuine open-weights commoditisation of capable models would compress the fixed cost of adoption and could offset the compliance step, as the open-source versus closed-source deployment dilemma already suggests is contested [E16] E61 . Fifth, regulatory divergence between the EU, US and China must not converge; a binding international standard would eliminate the multiplier on fixed compliance cost [E67] E6 . Sixth, enforcement capacity must continue to lag market dynamics [E24] E98 — a well-resourced, technically competent enforcement apparatus applied symmetrically to large incumbents would neutralise the moat, though nothing in the evidence base indicates such capacity currently exists. If four or more of these fail, the trajectory should be discarded rather than adjusted [E73]. Conclusion NomosX&#039;s position: the dominant public framing — &quot;AI regulation versus innovation&quot; — is novlangue [E91]. It conceals the two mechanisms that will actually determine the economic outcome, both of which are structural rather than substantive [E55]. The first is the discontinuity: tiered, threshold-triggered obligations generate a cliff at which firms rationally stop growing, stop reclassifying, and stop innovating [E91]. The single best-identified causal estimate available on this question is not about AI at all — it is the French administrative firm panel showing a sharp fall in the fraction of firms innovating just to the left of a size-contingent regulatory threshold, with reduced innovation responsiveness to exogenous demand shocks for firms below that threshold [E91] E91 . The AI Act is architecturally a size-and-tier-contingent regulation E83 . The mechanism ports. The second is the audit vacuum: the AI Act does not provide access to data for researchers and civil society, a regulatory gap in what would otherwise be a functioning third-party audit ecosystem [E59] E59 . A rule that cannot be independently verified does not produce trust; it produces documentation [E12]. This is the formalist trap that legal scholarship on the Brussels Effect routinely walks into — mistaking the diffusion of text for the diffusion of enforcement [E40]. The aggregate growth loss from AI regulation in Europe will be smaller than critics claim and the distributional loss larger than regulators admit [E40]. The reason is the shape of the AI-growth function: across 19 G20 countries from 2005 to 2023 using GMM with linear and quadratic terms, AI-related innovation has a positive and significant effect on economic growth, but the negative quadratic term confirms a concave relationship [E40] E40 . Concavity means the marginal social return to an additional unit of frontier AI capability is falling. If that is right, slowing the frontier by a few months costs less than is asserted — while blocking diffusion into the bottom of the firm distribution costs more [E101]. And diffusion is already the weak point: laggard firms in the bottom non établie of the productivity distribution converge toward the frontier more slowly in highly digital- and skill-intensive industries, indicating barriers to technology and knowledge diffusion [E101] E101 . Layer a threshold-contingent compliance regime on top of an already-broken diffusion channel and the result is not a slower frontier. It is a permanently thicker tail of non-adopters. The European market context sharpens the stakes rather than resolving them [E38]. The EU has lagged both quantitatively and qualitatively behind the US and China in growth over the last fifteen years while a new wave of high technology intensifies competition [E55] E55 , and EU-funded AI investment during 2014–2020 — around EUR 8 billion at NUTS2 level — went disproportionately to more developed regions, generating spillovers that reinforce existing regional specialization [E53] E53 . Regulatory burden and public investment are therefore pushing in the same direction: toward concentration [E26]. Three qualifications bound this position. First, the causal identification is borrowed, not native: E91 concerns French labor regulation at a 50-employee threshold, not AI conformity assessment, and the transfer is an analogy grounded in mechanism rather than a replication [E91]. Second, no source in this evidence base quantifies AI Act compliance cost per firm; that number is non établie, and its absence is a fact about the research, not about the regulation [E7]. Third, the counter-mechanism is real: well-specified rules can enable markets rather than suppress them, and the evidence that regulatory frameworks in the EU, US and UK currently fail to confront the challenges of generative AI [E38] E14 cuts against the assumption that the status quo ante was innovation-optimal [E8]. What Would Change Our Mind Four falsifiers, in order of force. First: firm-level microdata showing no discontinuity in AI system classification, deployment scope or firm growth at AI Act tier boundaries by end-2028 [E78]. That is the direct test of the ported threshold mechanism E91 , and its failure collapses the central claim. Second: replication studies overturning the concave AI-growth relationship [E40] E40 and establishing increasing returns at current capability levels — in which case frontier deceleration dominates diffusion failure and the whole cost accounting inverts. Third: enactment and enforcement of mandatory third-party model and data access closing the audit gap [E59] E59 , which would convert the AI Act from a documentation regime into a verification regime, restore the Brussels Effect, and make the compliance moat contestable. Fourth: evidence that regulatory sandboxes deliver measurable SME time-to-market reduction at scale, which would show the threshold pressure is being relieved precisely where it binds hardest [E59].
