Executive Summary

NomosX's position: the dominant public framing — "AI regulation versus innovation" — is novlangue [E91]. It conceals the two mechanisms that will actually determine the economic outcome, both of which are structural rather than substantive [E55]. The first is the discontinuity: tiered, threshold-triggered obligations generate a cliff at which firms rationally stop growing, stop reclassifying, and stop innovating [E91]. The single best-identified causal estimate available on this question is not about AI at all — it is the French administrative firm panel showing a sharp fall in the fraction of firms innovating just to the left of a size-contingent regulatory threshold, with reduced innovation responsiveness to exogenous demand shocks for firms below that threshold [E91]E91. The AI Act is architecturally a size-and-tier-contingent regulation E83. The mechanism ports. The second is the audit vacuum: the AI Act does not provide access to data for researchers and civil society, a regulatory gap in what would otherwise be a functioning third-party audit ecosystem [E59]E59. A rule that cannot be independently verified does not produce trust; it produces documentation [E12]. This is the formalist trap that legal scholarship on the Brussels Effect routinely walks into — mistaking the diffusion of text for the diffusion of enforcement [E40]. The aggregate growth loss from AI regulation in Europe will be smaller than critics claim and the distributional loss larger than regulators admit [E40]. The reason is the shape of the AI-growth function: across 19 G20 countries from 2005 to 2023 using GMM with linear and quadratic terms, AI-related innovation has a positive and significant effect on economic growth, but the negative quadratic term confirms a concave relationship [E40]E40. Concavity means the marginal social return to an additional unit of frontier AI capability is falling. If that is right, slowing the frontier by a few months costs less than is asserted — while blocking diffusion into the bottom of the firm distribution costs more [E101]. And diffusion is already the weak point: laggard firms in the bottom non établie of the productivity distribution converge toward the frontier more slowly in highly digital- and skill-intensive industries, indicating barriers to technology and knowledge diffusion [E101]E101. Layer a threshold-contingent compliance regime on top of an already-broken diffusion channel and the result is not a slower frontier. It is a permanently thicker tail of non-adopters. The European market context sharpens the stakes rather than resolving them [E38]. The EU has lagged both quantitatively and qualitatively behind the US and China in growth over the last fifteen years while a new wave of high technology intensifies competition [E55]E55, and EU-funded AI investment during 2014–2020 — around EUR 8 billion at NUTS2 level — went disproportionately to more developed regions, generating spillovers that reinforce existing regional specialization [E53]E53. Regulatory burden and public investment are therefore pushing in the same direction: toward concentration [E26]. Three qualifications bound this position. First, the causal identification is borrowed, not native: E91 concerns French labor regulation at a 50-employee threshold, not AI conformity assessment, and the transfer is an analogy grounded in mechanism rather than a replication [E91]. Second, no source in this evidence base quantifies AI Act compliance cost per firm; that number is non établie, and its absence is a fact about the research, not about the regulation [E7]. Third, the counter-mechanism is real: well-specified rules can enable markets rather than suppress them, and the evidence that regulatory frameworks in the EU, US and UK currently fail to confront the challenges of generative AI [E38]E14 cuts against the assumption that the status quo ante was innovation-optimal [E8]. Confidence: tempered, not firm.

Literature Review

Four anchors carry this analysis, and they differ in evidential weight [E100]. The strongest is quasi-experimental. A tractable, quantifiable endogenous growth model applied to population administrative firm panel data from France — where many labor regulations bind at 50 or more employees — finds nonparametrically a sharp fall in the fraction of firms innovating just to the left of the regulatory threshold, and a dynamic reduction in firms' innovation response to exogenous demand shocks below the threshold [E91]E91. This is the only source in the base offering credible causal identification of regulation's effect on innovation [E40]. The second is textual and institutional. The protection of fundamental rights and the human-centric, ethical and responsible use of AI is the central ambition of European AI strategy [E83]E83; the Commission's proposal has been assessed against the standard of legally trustworthy AI and found wanting on specific counts [E10]E86; and the Act, alongside the Digital Services Act, does not provide access to information on models and data for researchers and civil society, which constitutes an identified regulatory gap in the intended audit ecosystem [E59]E59. Enforcement capability, not obligation stringency, is the binding variable — a point reinforced by the observation from digital competition scholarship that effective enforcement must keep pace with market dynamics if genuine innovation and choice are to prevail [E98]E98. The third is the growth function itself. AI-related innovation has a positive and significant effect on economic growth in G20 economies, with a negative quadratic term confirming concavity [E40]E40; AI functions as a general-purpose technology aiding production, marketing and customer acquisition, increasing firm productivity and consumer reach [E50]E50; and in China, 20,178 firm-year observations from 2007–2022 show AI innovation operating through governance-mediated affordance actualisation in high-growth firms [E29]E29. Sector-level magnitudes exist where costs are measurable: wider AI adoption in US healthcare could yield savings of 5–10 percent of national health spending, roughly USD 200–360 billion annually [E54]E54; financial institutions' AI expenditure is projected to double to USD 97 billion by 2027 at a 29 [E8].6 percent CAGR E8. The fourth is diffusion. Laggard firms in the bottom 40 percent of the productivity distribution converge more slowly toward the frontier precisely in digital- and skill-intensive industries [E101]E101, while AI adoption itself exhibits epidemic diffusion properties in which early-stage patterns establish path-dependencies shaping the distribution of technological opportunity and socio-economic returns [E68]E68. The threshold mechanism and the diffusion mechanism compound. [E54]

Emerging Trends

By 2030 the European AI market will be structurally bimodal [E40]. At the top, a small number of firms operating across all three major regulatory blocs will have internalised compliance as a standing function, absorbing the cost of divergent EU, US and Chinese priorities on transparency, fairness and privacy [E8]E6 and treating the AI Act's conformity regime as a barrier that protects their position rather than constrains it E98. At the bottom, a thickening tail of firms will remain below classification triggers by design — restricting deployment scope, capping functionality, avoiding high-risk domains — reproducing at AI-tier boundaries the bunching already documented at labour-law size thresholds in France [E57]E91. The middle thins. Three structural changes follow. First, an assurance industry consolidates. Responsible AI architecture, governance frameworks and regulatory convergence services move from consultancy offerings to standing enterprise functions [E70]E70; certification labels become the consumer-facing interface of audit, tested against end-user comprehension rather than technical adequacy E49; and in healthcare and finance the operative governance instrument is contractual — data usage agreements, master service provider contracts, indemnification clauses E16 — not statutory. Regulation is privatised at the point of application. Second, the audit ecosystem gap becomes politically salient. The absence of researcher and civil-society data access E59 will be exposed by the first serious enforcement failure in a high-risk domain [E58]. The most likely repair path is not statutory amendment but the construction of independent observation infrastructures, on the model by which citizen-generated data has been translated into evidence for shaping policy [E58]E58 and by which networks of citizen observatories increase the quality and quantity of independently generated monitoring data [E84]E84. Expect proposals for an AI observatory network with legally mandated model access; expect industry to resist it more strongly than it resisted the Act itself, because access is the only obligation that cannot be satisfied on paper [E55]. Third, geoeconomic divergence hardens. Digital sovereignty discourse justifies state regulation and delimitation of data flows across very different contexts [E80]E80, and in the EU specifically it operates as regulatory mercantilism responding to perceived loss of competitiveness and dependence on foreign infrastructures [E95]E95. Against a backdrop of fifteen years of EU underperformance relative to the US and China [E8]E55 and EU AI funding concentrated in already-developed regions E53, the political economy will push toward strategic exemptions, national champions and sandbox carve-outs — instruments that relieve the threshold pressure for selected firms while leaving it intact for everyone else, which worsens the distributional outcome while improving the headline one. Quantified expectation, stated as falsifiable: the AI Act's aggregate effect on EU GDP growth over this horizon will not be separately identifiable from macroeconomic noise, while firm-level microdata will show a statistically detectable discontinuity in AI deployment scope at tier boundaries. Both halves must hold for the position to stand.

Conclusion

NomosX's position: the dominant public framing — "AI regulation versus innovation" — is novlangue [E91]. It conceals the two mechanisms that will actually determine the economic outcome, both of which are structural rather than substantive [E55]. The first is the discontinuity: tiered, threshold-triggered obligations generate a cliff at which firms rationally stop growing, stop reclassifying, and stop innovating [E91]. The single best-identified causal estimate available on this question is not about AI at all — it is the French administrative firm panel showing a sharp fall in the fraction of firms innovating just to the left of a size-contingent regulatory threshold, with reduced innovation responsiveness to exogenous demand shocks for firms below that threshold [E91]E91. The AI Act is architecturally a size-and-tier-contingent regulation E83. The mechanism ports. The second is the audit vacuum: the AI Act does not provide access to data for researchers and civil society, a regulatory gap in what would otherwise be a functioning third-party audit ecosystem [E59]E59. A rule that cannot be independently verified does not produce trust; it produces documentation [E12]. This is the formalist trap that legal scholarship on the Brussels Effect routinely walks into — mistaking the diffusion of text for the diffusion of enforcement [E40]. The aggregate growth loss from AI regulation in Europe will be smaller than critics claim and the distributional loss larger than regulators admit [E40]. The reason is the shape of the AI-growth function: across 19 G20 countries from 2005 to 2023 using GMM with linear and quadratic terms, AI-related innovation has a positive and significant effect on economic growth, but the negative quadratic term confirms a concave relationship [E40]E40. Concavity means the marginal social return to an additional unit of frontier AI capability is falling. If that is right, slowing the frontier by a few months costs less than is asserted — while blocking diffusion into the bottom of the firm distribution costs more [E101]. And diffusion is already the weak point: laggard firms in the bottom non établie of the productivity distribution converge toward the frontier more slowly in highly digital- and skill-intensive industries, indicating barriers to technology and knowledge diffusion [E101]E101. Layer a threshold-contingent compliance regime on top of an already-broken diffusion channel and the result is not a slower frontier. It is a permanently thicker tail of non-adopters. The European market context sharpens the stakes rather than resolving them [E38]. The EU has lagged both quantitatively and qualitatively behind the US and China in growth over the last fifteen years while a new wave of high technology intensifies competition [E55]E55, and EU-funded AI investment during 2014–2020 — around EUR 8 billion at NUTS2 level — went disproportionately to more developed regions, generating spillovers that reinforce existing regional specialization [E53]E53. Regulatory burden and public investment are therefore pushing in the same direction: toward concentration [E26]. Three qualifications bound this position. First, the causal identification is borrowed, not native: E91 concerns French labor regulation at a 50-employee threshold, not AI conformity assessment, and the transfer is an analogy grounded in mechanism rather than a replication [E91]. Second, no source in this evidence base quantifies AI Act compliance cost per firm; that number is non établie, and its absence is a fact about the research, not about the regulation [E7]. Third, the counter-mechanism is real: well-specified rules can enable markets rather than suppress them, and the evidence that regulatory frameworks in the EU, US and UK currently fail to confront the challenges of generative AI [E38]E14 cuts against the assumption that the status quo ante was innovation-optimal [E8]. Confidence: tempered, not firm.

Open Questions

• No source in this base quantifies AI Act compliance cost per firm, per risk tier, or as a share of R&D spend [E40]. This number is non établie. Every claim about the magnitude of the compliance burden — including this analysis's — is therefore directional, not quantified [E20].
• No source measures EU regulatory sandbox throughput, SME participation, graduation-to-deployment rates, or time-to-market effects [E50]. The second-largest mechanism in the Pass 1 friction map is empirically unobserved [E3].
• No firm-level data on AI system classification decisions under the Act exist yet, which means the central prediction of this analysis — bunching at tier boundaries — is currently untestable and will remain so until conformity filing data are published [E31].
• The benefit side of the Regulatory Impact Assessment is systematically unmeasured [E8]. Standardised metrics for the burden of information harms are themselves identified as absent, requiring a common vocabulary, evidence review and technical working group before quantification is possible [E26]E26; the same measurement problem applies to avoided algorithmic discrimination and preserved due process.
• No source establishes whether explainability requirements impose a genuine performance cost [E50]. The healthcare explainability literature frames the question as medical, legal, ethical and societal rather than purely technological [E74]E74, and evaluation metrics for generative healthcare systems are proposed but not yet implemented, validated or benchmarked [E10]E10. The transparency-performance trade-off is asserted more often than it is measured [E91].
• The analytic-perspective problem is unresolved and consequential [E58]. Health economics establishes that the choice of perspective directly influences which costs enter the numerator, and that broadening the perspective can either decrease or increase the resulting ratio depending on context [E7]E7. Applied to AI regulation: whether the Act 'costs' growth depends entirely on whose costs are counted — developer, deployer, worker, or society [E7]. No AI Regulatory Impact Assessment in this base states its perspective explicitly [E70].
• Enforcement capacity data are absent. Formalist analysis of statutory text tells us nothing about member-state notified-body capacity, technical competence, or willingness to act against large providers — the variable on which the compliance-moat mechanism most depends E98.
• Labour-market transmission is under-evidenced. Automation is characterised as labour-saving, resource-saving and productive of winner-takes-all dynamics [E73]E73, and generative AI's workplace benefits are expected to be unevenly distributed [E14]E14, but no source in this base links AI regulation specifically to employment outcomes [E18].
• The offshored data-work layer that underpins AI supply chains — procured via arm's-length contracts and marketplace platforms across France, Madagascar and Venezuela [E57]E57 — falls almost entirely outside the AI Act's territorial and substantive scope [E83]. The regulation governs deployment while the labour and extraction occur elsewhere; this asymmetry is unquantified and structurally important.

What Would Change Our Mind

Four falsifiers, in order of force. First: firm-level microdata showing no discontinuity in AI system classification, deployment scope or firm growth at AI Act tier boundaries by end-2028 [E78]. That is the direct test of the ported threshold mechanism E91, and its failure collapses the central claim. Second: replication studies overturning the concave AI-growth relationship [E40]E40 and establishing increasing returns at current capability levels — in which case frontier deceleration dominates diffusion failure and the whole cost accounting inverts. Third: enactment and enforcement of mandatory third-party model and data access closing the audit gap [E59]E59, which would convert the AI Act from a documentation regime into a verification regime, restore the Brussels Effect, and make the compliance moat contestable. Fourth: evidence that regulatory sandboxes deliver measurable SME time-to-market reduction at scale, which would show the threshold pressure is being relieved precisely where it binds hardest [E59]. Any one of the first three would require substantial revision; the first two would require abandonment [E89].
