Probable futures

Base case: Trust-driven competitiveness · base 91%
The EU AI Act becomes a global benchmark, fostering user trust and legal certainty that accelerate adoption. New markets in compliance, auditing, and AI infrastructure flourish. European firms, while facing upfront costs, gain a reputation for safe AI that differentiates them in global markets.
Adverse case: Compliance drag and innovation flight 9%
Compliance costs disproportionately burden startups and SMEs, driving talent and investment to less-regulated markets like the US and China. Large incumbents capture the market, reducing competition. Europe becomes a laggard in high-risk AI applications.

Institute position

NomosX holds that the EU AI Act, despite imposing short-term compliance costs, ultimately enhances European economic competitiveness by establishing a trust-based framework that drives adoption, spawns high-growth regtech and GPU-as-a-service markets, and positions the EU as a global regulatory standard-setter. The evidence shows that 65% of European tech companies value the legal clarity and user trust generated by the regulation 5, while regtech markets are projected to grow at over 20% CAGR 9 and GPUaaS at nearly 30% 10, indicating new growth vectors. However, this positive verdict is conditional: the regulation disproportionately burdens SMEs (45% of companies report increased costs 5), and Europe controls only ~10% of semiconductor supply chain value 6, creating a strategic vulnerability. Moreover, environmental trade-offs are largely unaddressed 6. The net effect hinges on whether supportive policies—such as AI infrastructure investments and SME compliance assistance—are implemented rapidly. With medium confidence (64%), we conclude that the EU's regulatory leadership provides a first-mover advantage in trustworthy AI, but failure to mitigate SME burdens and hardware dependencies could cede market leadership to the US and China.

Confidence 64/100 · medium

Strongest objections

  • The AI Act imposes a significant compliance burden that harms innovation, especially for smaller firms, without proven offsetting benefits.
  • The EU's weak position in semiconductor supply chains means it cannot capitalize on AI hardware demands, limiting the upside of regulation-driven markets.
  • Environmental and social risks from AI are poorly addressed, potentially eroding the trust that the regulation aims to build.

What would change our mind

  • Data showing a sustained decline in European AI startups or venture capital funding relative to the US and China over the next 3-5 years.
  • Evidence that the majority of European companies report net negative impact on competitiveness from the AI Act after 2026.
  • Adoption of substantially different AI regulatory frameworks by key trading partners (e.g., US or Japan) that contradict EU standards, undermining the EU's standard-setting role.
