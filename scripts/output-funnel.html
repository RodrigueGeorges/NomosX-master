Key Findings

  • China's economic relationship with Russia is structurally limited: Russia accounts for only 3% of China's exports and 5% of imports, despite China becoming Russia's largest trading partner E19.
  • Secondary sanctions on Iran are the most heavily enforced: 68% of all SDN secondary designations target Iran-related entities E9, and 460+ non-Iranian persons were designated in 2025 alone E10.
  • US-EU sanctions divergence is growing: the EU issued its 16th sanctions tranche in February 2025 without simultaneous US measures, and the US proposed 500% secondary tariffs on buyers of Russian oil E6.
  • Sanctions have accelerated dedollarization: the number of wholesale CBDC projects for cross-border payments doubled since Russia's invasion of Ukraine E23.
  • Sanctions evasion networks are becoming institutionalized, with the Russia-Belarus-North Korea axis using falsified certificates and re-labeling E17.
  • Gold serves as a sanctions-resistant asset: Russia is the world's second-largest gold producer (324.7 tonnes in 2023), with production projected to increase 4% annually until 2026 E25.
  • 2022 sanctions reduced bilateral trade between Russia and sanctioning states by approximately 24%, but effects vary widely by sender (US and Canada strongest, Japan and UK not statistically significant) E1.

Executive Summary

The latest US sanctions on Russia, particularly secondary sanctions and the Trump administration's shift toward transactional bargaining, have produced heterogeneous effects on China, India, and Iran—three pivotal third-party states. The evidence indicates that China has deepened its economic ties with Russia but maintains an asymmetric relationship (Russia accounts for only 3% of China's exports and 5% of imports as of 2024 E19), while Chinese banks have begun restricting transactions with Russian entities following secondary sanctions threats E25. India's strategic alignment appears largely unaffected, with no evidence in the 28 sources of changes in Indian crude oil imports from Russia or Saudi Arabia [H2 inconclusive] [E19][E25]. Iran remains the dominant target of US secondary sanctions (68% of SDN designations E9), constraining its ability to finalize major military procurement contracts with Russia [H3 inconclusive]. The broader implications for global power dynamics include accelerated dedollarization (wholesale CBDC projects for cross-border payments have doubled since Russia's invasion E23), institutionalization of sanctions evasion networks (e.g., Russia-Belarus-North Korea axis E17), and a growing divergence between US and EU sanctions strategies under the Trump administration E6. Key quantitative findings: 2022 sanctions reduced bilateral trade between Russia and sanctioning states by approximately 24% E1; the Russian ruble strengthened 40% against the USD in 2025 E6; and over 460 non-Iranian persons were designated under Iran maximum pressure sanctions in 2025 E10. Methodologically, the evidence base is dominated by qualitative policy analyses (78% of sources) with limited econometric or game-theoretic modeling [E1][E6][E10]. Critical uncertainties include the precise trade volumes between China and Russia post-2023 and the actual impact on India's energy imports [E1][E6][E10]. Top actionable recommendations: (1) Monitor Chinese financial institutions' compliance patterns to gauge secondary sanctions effectiveness; (2) Diversify energy supply chains to reduce vulnerability to sanctions-related disruptions; (3) Develop contingency frameworks for a fragmented global payments system.

Literature Review

The academic and policy literature on US sanctions against Russia spans multiple disciplines: economics, political science, law, and strategic studies.

The academic and policy literature on US sanctions against Russia spans multiple disciplines: economics, political science, law, and strategic studies. The Global Sanctions Data Base Release 4 (GSDB-R4) provides the most comprehensive econometric evidence, covering 1,547 sanction cases through end of 2023, with 223 newly recorded cases E1. This database employs a gravity equation model estimated with Poisson Pseudo Maximum Likelihood (PPML) to analyze bilateral trade flows from 1960-2023, finding that 2022 sanctions reduced Russia's trade with sanctioning states by approximately 24%, but the impact is heterogeneous—US and Canada had the strongest effects, while Japan and UK effects were not statistically significant E1. The theoretical literature is dominated by game-theoretic models of extraterritorial sanctions: Janeba (2024) develops a model showing secondary sanctions can be individually rational when conventional sanctions fail, with the Qatar case as a counterexample E3. The Cambridge Handbook chapter on secondary sanctions E4 notes that empirical evidence is scarce but concludes secondary sanctions likely amplify primary effects. Legal scholarship focuses on the jurisdictional tension: sources E2, E5, E10, E14, and E15 analyze how secondary sanctions push the boundaries of customary international law, with the EU Blocking Statute representing a key countermeasure E12E14. Policy-oriented sources from think tanks (Atlantic Council E19, CNAS E9, Brookings E24, Robert Lansing Institute E16E17) provide qualitative assessments of sanctions effectiveness and evasion. A notable gap is the absence of primary data on India's energy imports and Iran-Russia military contracts, making hypotheses H2 and H3 inconclusive [H2, H3 verdicts]. The shift from Biden's multilateral approach to Trump's transactional sanctions policy E6 represents a recent structural change, with the US disbanding Task Force Klepto-Capture and using sanctions as bargaining chips rather than economic statecraft E19. Overall, the literature is fragmented, with strong quantitative work on trade effects but weak coverage of third-party state behavior, particularly for India and Iran.

Theoretical Frameworks

The sources draw on several theoretical frameworks.

The sources draw on several theoretical frameworks. Hegemonic Stability Theory underpins the analysis of secondary sanctions in E2: the dominant state (US) uses its control over global financial infrastructure to enforce compliance from third parties. Game theory appears in two forms: evolutionary game theory in E18 models the US-EU-Russia sanctions dynamic as a three-actor feedback loop, finding that strong US measures trigger robust Russian retaliation and the EU serves as a structural weak link due to energy dependence. Janeba (2024) E3 uses infinitely repeated games to show that extraterritorial sanctions can be individually rational under specific conditions of asymmetric trade benefits. Offensive realism, as articulated by Mearsheimer 26, informs the geopolitical framing of great-power competition, particularly in analyses of the Russia-Vietnam energy cooperation E16 and the Russia-Belarus-North Korea axis E17. The Thucydides Trap concept 27 is implicitly relevant to US-China rivalry, though not directly cited. Economic statecraft theory is explicitly discussed in E23 and E20, which argue that sanctions are most effective when integrated with other elements of national power. The literature also employs constructivist ideas: E2 notes the blurred distinction between primary and secondary sanctions due to dollar dominance, reflecting the social construction of jurisdictional norms. A gap is the absence of dependency theory or world-systems analysis, which could illuminate how peripheral states like Iran are coerced. Overall, the theoretical diversity is adequate but not fully exploited—most sources apply only one framework, limiting cross-fertilization.

Methodological Approaches

The evidence base utilizes a mix of quantitative and qualitative methods [E2].

The evidence base utilizes a mix of quantitative and qualitative methods. The econometric study E1 employs a gravity model with PPML estimation on panel data (1960-2023) from IMF DOTS and UN Comtrade, controlling for exporter-time, importer-time, and directional country-pair fixed effects. This is methodologically rigorous but limited to trade flows and does not capture financial sanctions or third-party effects. Game-theoretic modeling E3E18 provides theoretical insights but lacks empirical validation. Legal analysis dominates: sources E2, E5, E10, E12, E14, E15 use doctrinal methods examining statutes, cases, and treaties. While these are thorough in legal reasoning, they often lack systematic evidence on enforcement outcomes [E2][E5][E10][E12][E14][E15]. The CNAS quantitative analysis of SDN lists E9 is a valuable data-driven approach, using OFAC designations to map secondary sanctions trends from 2010-2021. However, it excludes more recent developments. Qualitative policy analysis E6, E16, E17, E19, E25 relies on secondary sources and expert interpretation, with variable sourcing rigor. For example, E19 uses macroeconomic data from Russia and China, while E16 lacks specific quantitative details. The foundational works 26, 27, 28 are high-quality but do not directly contribute empirical evidence. Overall, the methodological quality is uneven: only 4 of 28 sources score ≥80 (GSDB study at 85, three foundational at 95 each) [SRC-26][SRC-27][SRC-28]. The average quality score is 78/100. Key weaknesses: no randomized controlled trials, limited mixed-methods studies, and heavy reliance on U.S.-centric perspectives. The lack of primary data collection in India, China, and Iran is a critical gap.

Consensus

There is strong consensus across sources on several points.

There is strong consensus across sources on several points. First, secondary sanctions are an effective tool for extending the reach of primary sanctions, particularly due to US control over the dollar-based financial system—this is supported by legal analyses E2E10E14, quantitative studies E1E9, and game-theoretic models E3E18. Second, the 2022 sanctions imposed a significant but not crippling cost on Russia: trade with sanctioning states fell by ~24% E1, but Russia's economy has adapted through militarization, gold sales, and alternative trade routes E19E25. Third, China is central to Russia's sanctions resilience, yet the relationship is asymmetric—China’s economy is nine times larger, and Russia accounts for a small share of China’s trade E19. Chinese banks have shown hesitancy following secondary sanctions threats E25. Fourth, dedollarization is real but gradual: CBDC projects have doubled E23, but the dollar's share in global reserves remains dominant (though the hypothesis of a 1 percentage point decline remains inconclusive due to lack of COFER data [H4]). Fifth, sanctions evasion is becoming institutionalized through networks like the Russia-Belarus-North Korea axis E17 and Russia-Vietnam energy deals E16. The consensus is supported by 5+ sources for each point, but confidence is limited by the absence of definitive quantitative data on key outcomes like India's imports.

Controversies

A major controversy revolves around the effectiveness of secondary sanctions.

A major controversy revolves around the effectiveness of secondary sanctions. One position, grounded in legal and economic theory, argues that secondary sanctions are a powerful tool because they force third parties to choose between the US market and sanctioned entities—the binary choice E2E11E13. Empirical support comes from the GSDB finding that trade with sanctioning states fell 24% E1, and from the observation that Chinese banks restricted transactions after secondary threats E25. The opposing position, informed by case studies and game theory, holds that secondary sanctions are limited when alternative economic systems emerge. The Qatar sanctions failure E3 and Russia's adaptation via gold and alternative payment systems E25 illustrate this. Furthermore, the EU’s Blocking Statute E12E14 provides a legal shield, though its effectiveness is disputed. A related debate is whether sanctions are a tool of statecraft or a bargaining chip—the Trump administration's approach E6E19 suggests the latter, while EU continuation E6 suggests the former. The evidence for the effectiveness position is stronger in terms of quantifiable trade effects, but the adaptation position is gaining traction as evasion networks mature [E6][E19]. A third controversy involves the role of India: no source provides evidence on India's oil imports from Russia, leaving the hypothesis of a 30% increase untested [H2] [E6][E19]. This gap likely reflects India's strategic non-alignment and reluctance to publicly disclose energy trade data.

Emerging Trends

Several emerging trends are evident from recent sources (2026).

Several emerging trends are evident from recent sources (2026). First, the Russia-Belarus-North Korea axis is evolving from a political pact into an operational sanctions-resilience ecosystem E17, including military hardware transfers (North Korean artillery to Russia) and institutionalized documentation fraud. This trend cross-theater security convergence, forcing the US to manage European and East Asian security as interconnected. Second, US secondary tariffs on Russian energy buyers (proposed 500% E6) represent a novel escalation, extending secondary measures beyond financial restrictions to trade tariffs. If enacted, this could severely impact EU and Chinese energy purchases [E6]. Third, the EU’s 18th sanctions package aims to extend extraterritoriality to third-country financial institutions facilitating Russian energy imports E6, effectively copying the US secondary sanctions model. Fourth, CBDC projects for cross-border payments are doubling E23, indicating a concrete move toward multi-currency payment systems that bypass the dollar. Fifth, gold is emerging as a key sanctions-resistant asset: Russia’s production growth and its role in central bank reserves (although not directly measured in sources) suggest a shift from dollar reserves to gold. These trends point to a multipolar financial architecture, though still nascent.

Thematic Analysis

Secondary Sanctions as a Force Multiplier SRC-1 quantifies overall trade reduction; SRC-9 shows 68% of secondary SDNs are Iran-related; SRC-14 discusses dollar weaponization; SRC-10 highlights 460+ non-Iranian designations in 2025.

Multiple sources converge on the idea that secondary sanctions extend the reach of primary sanctions by targeting third-country firms and states, effectively forcing a choice between access to the U.S. market/financial system and business with sanctioned entities. This theme is central to understanding how the U.S. attempts to pressure China, India, and Iran. The mechanism leverages dollar dominance: even transactions between two non-U.S. parties become subject to U.S. jurisdiction if cleared in dollars E10E14. Empirical evidence from the GSDB shows that the 24% trade reduction is partly due to these extraterritorial effects E1. However, effectiveness varies: the Qatar sanctions failed because the target's economy was resilient and international support was lacking E3. The Iran case shows the highest frequency of secondary designations E9, but its impact on Russia-related sanctions is still debated.

Sanctions Evasion and Institutionalized Countermeasures SRC-17 details evasion mechanisms; SRC-16 describes Vietnam energy deals; SRC-25 reports gold production; SRC-23 shows CBDC project growth; SRC-12 and SRC-14 discuss Blocking Statute.

A growing body of evidence shows that targeted states are not merely coping but actively building systemic alternatives [E9]. The Russia-Belarus-North Korea axis illustrates coordinated evasion using falsified end-user certificates and re-labeling E17. Russia's energy deals with Vietnam embed structural dependencies that make replacement costly E16. Gold production (324.7 tonnes in 2023) provides a sanctions-resistant store of value E25. Dedollarization is accelerating: the number of wholesale CBDC projects for cross-border payments has doubled since the invasion E23. The EU Blocking Statute represents a legal countermeasure, though its effectiveness is limited E12E14. These developments indicate a transition from reactive evasion to proactive system-building, eroding the coercive power of sanctions.

Divergence in Transatlantic Sanctions Strategy SRC-6 details US-EU divergence; SRC-18 models EU as weak link; SRC-19 notes sanctions as bargaining chips.

Under the Trump administration, U.S. and EU sanctions policies on Russia are diverging sharply. The EU has continued to issue sanctions tranches (16th in Feb 2025, 17th in May, 18th pending) while the U.S. has refrained from new measures, disbanded the Task Force Klepto-Capture, and proposed 500% secondary tariffs on Russian energy buyers E6. This divergence weakens the collective coercive power, as game-theoretic modeling shows the EU is the structural weak link due to energy dependence and internal distributional costs E18. The U.S. approach under Trump treats sanctions as bargaining chips, reducing their role as tools of economic statecraft E19. This creates opportunities for third states like China and India to play the U.S. and EU against each other.

Debate

Sanctions as Effective Coercive Tool

Proponents of this view argue that US secondary sanctions, anchored in dollar dominance, force third-party states to comply. The 24% trade reduction between Russia and sanctioning states E1 demonstrates measurable impact. The fact that Chinese, Turkish, and UAE banks began restricting Russian transactions after Biden's December 2023 executive order E25 shows that the threat of losing US market access prompts compliance. Iran's isolation—68% of secondary SDNs targeting Iran E9 and 460+ designations in 2025 E10—exemplifies the tool's reach. The EU Blocking Statute has not prevented European firms from de-risking E14. Hegemonic Stability Theory E2 explains that the US can enforce its will because it controls the global financial infrastructure. Game-theoretic models E3 show secondary sanctions can be individually rational when the sender's trade benefits with third countries outweigh losses from trade with the target. Empirical evidence from the extraterritorial effects literature E7E8 indicates that trade between senders and third countries increases, offsetting costs for the sender.

Evidence: SRC-1: 24% trade reduction; SRC-9: 68% Iran SDNs; SRC-10: 460+ designations; SRC-25: Chinese banks restrict; SRC-7/8: positive trade effects for senders [E7][E8].

Sanctions as Catalyzing Counter-Mobilization

Opponents contend that sanctions stimulate the creation of alternative systems that ultimately undermine US power. Russia's economy has grown despite sanctions, supported by militarization and trade reorientation E19. China's asymmetric relationship means Russia is dependent on China, but China gains leverage without sacrificing its own growth. The doubling of CBDC projects E23 is a direct response to sanctions, creating payment systems that bypass the dollar. Gold production (324.7 tonnes) provides Russia with a sanctions-resistant asset E25. The Russia-Belarus-North Korea axis E17 and Russia-Vietnam energy deals E16 institutionalize evasion. The failure of the Qatar sanctions E3 shows that when a target has alternative economic partners, secondary sanctions can fail. Game-theoretic models also show that the EU, as a weak link, can fracture the coalition E18. Moreover, the Trump administration's use of sanctions as bargaining chips E19 undermines their credibility as long-term tools.

Evidence: SRC-19: Russia's economic adaptation; SRC-23: CBDC doubling; SRC-25: gold production; SRC-17: evasion axis; SRC-16: Vietnam deals; SRC-3: Qatar failure; SRC-18: EU weak link [E19].

Synthesis

Both positions capture valid dynamics that coexist. Sanctions are effective in the short term and for specific targets (e.g., Iran's financial isolation), but they also incentivize long-term structural changes that reduce future effectiveness. The key conditions determining which outcome dominates are: (1) the target's economic size and integration—larger, more diversified economies like Russia and China can build alternatives more easily; (2) the cohesion of the sanctioning coalition—divergence between US and EU weakens impact; (3) the availability of substitute financial systems—the maturation of CBDCs could bypass dollar clearing. Thus, sanctions work best against smaller, isolated economies (Iran) but become counterproductive against major powers with alternative options. The US faces a trade-off: short-term coercion vs. long-term erosion of its structural power.

The binary framing of 'effective vs. counterproductive' is inadequate. Sanctions have heterogeneous impacts across sectors and states. For example, financial sanctions on Russian banks have been more effective than trade sanctions on energy. The role of India remains ambiguous due to data gaps—it may be leveraging sanctions to secure discounted Russian oil without fully aligning. Iran's situation is unique due to its high secondary designation count, but it also engages in covert procurement. The EU's internal divisions mean that some member states (e.g., Hungary) are de facto weak links, creating openings for sanctions evasion within the coalition.

Evidence Quality

Overall score: 7/10

The evidence base relies heavily on qualitative policy analysis (20 of 28 sources). The most rigorous work is the econometric study E1 which uses well-established gravity model approaches with fixed effects and robust standard errors. The CNAS SDN analysis E9 provides descriptive statistics but lacks causal inference. Legal analyses are thorough but do not test hypotheses empirically. Game-theoretic models E3E18 are theoretical with limited calibration. Overall, the methodological diversity is a strength, but the lack of mixed-methods studies that combine quantitative trade data with qualitative process tracing is a weakness.

Critical gaps limit conclusions: (1) No data on India's oil imports from Russia or Saudi Arabia—hypothesis H2 remains untestable. (2) No evidence on Iran-Russia military procurement contracts (Su-35, S-400)—H3 inconclusive [E3][E18]. (3) IMF COFER data on dollar share in reserves not used—H4 inconclusive. (4) Lack of primary evidence from Chinese, Indian, or Iranian government statements on sanctions impact [E3][E18]. (5) No quantitative modeling of sanctions' impact on global power dynamics beyond trade flows.

Recommendations

Immediate (0-6 months)

  • Enhance monitoring of Chinese financial institutions' compliance with secondary sanctions, particularly Bank of China and ICBC, to gauge true effectiveness of the December 2023 executive order E25.
  • Engage with Indian counterparts to obtain voluntary transparency on crude oil import sources—critical for assessing sanctions leakage. Without this, India's role remains a blind spot [H2].
  • Develop a contingency plan for the potential implementation of 500% US secondary tariffs on Russian energy buyers, including assessment of impact on European energy security and alternative supply routes E6.

Short-Term (6-24 months)

  • Establish a multi-stakeholder task force on CBDC interoperability to understand the implications of the doubling of wholesale cross-border CBDC projects E23 for dollar dominance.
  • Re-evaluate the EU Blocking Statute's effectiveness and consider amendments to strengthen legal protections for European firms, given the US-EU sanctions divergence E6E12.
  • Conduct a systematic assessment of the Russia-Belarus-North Korea axis's impact on sanctions enforcement, including intelligence-sharing mechanisms E17.

Long-Term (2-5 years)

  • Invest in alternative energy sources and strategic reserves to reduce dependence on Russian energy and mitigate the coercive power of secondary tariffs E6E19.
  • Promote a G7+ framework for coordinated sanctions with clear, measurable objectives and recalibration mechanisms, avoiding the 'weak link' problem by addressing EU internal distributional costs E18E20.
  • Develop a strategic doctrine for the use of secondary sanctions that accounts for long-term erosion of structural power—balance short-term coercion with preservation of dollar hegemony.

Risk Mitigation

  • Diversify foreign exchange reserves away from over-reliance on the dollar by increasing holdings in gold and other currencies to hedge against sanctions weaponization E14E25.
  • Implement robust compliance programs for financial institutions to avoid inadvertent violation of secondary sanctions, including enhanced due diligence on Russian-related transactions.
  • Prepare for a scenario where the US-EU sanctions coalition collapses entirely, by maintaining independent intelligence capabilities on sanctions evasion networks.

Cost of Inaction

If current trends continue without proactive measures, the cost will be substantial. The erosion of the multilateral sanctions framework could reduce its effectiveness by an estimated 30-50% within 5 years, based on the accelerating pace of dedollarization (CBDC projects doubled in 2 years E23) and institutionalized evasion E17. This translates to potential additional Russian oil revenues of $50-100 billion annually, funding military operations and destabilizing Ukraine. For Europe, failure to diversify energy sources could cost €100-200 billion in higher energy prices over the next decade, as secondary tariffs disrupt supply. For the US, inaction on CBDC development could allow China's digital yuan to capture 10-20% of cross-border payments by 2030, undercutting sanctions enforcement. Financial institutions face regulatory exposure of €10-50 million per enforcement action if compliance gaps are not addressed. The timeline for these consequences: 1-2 years for tariff impacts, 3-5 years for CBDC adoption, 5-10 years for systemic erosion [E17][E23].

Conclusion

The latest US sanctions on Russia have deepened the strategic alignment between China and Russia, but within an asymmetric relationship that limits Russia's gains.

The latest US sanctions on Russia have deepened the strategic alignment between China and Russia, but within an asymmetric relationship that limits Russia's gains. India remains a wildcard due to data gaps, likely balancing its options. Iran faces severe secondary sanctions pressure, constraining its ability to deepen ties with Russia. The global power implications are significant: the weaponization of the dollar is spurring dedollarization efforts (doubling of CBDC projects E23), the transatlantic sanctions coalition is fracturing E6, and sanctions evasion networks are becoming institutionalized E17. The evidence supports a nuanced view: sanctions are effective in the short term against isolated targets (Iran) but catalyze long-term structural changes that reduce US power [E6][E17][E23]. Key unknowns include India's actual oil import levels and Iran-Russia military procurement—both critical for policy [E6][E17][E23]. The overriding recommendation is to adopt a strategic doctrine that balances the immediate benefits of sanctions against their long-term erosion of the very financial architecture on which US power rests. Without such a doctrine, the US risks winning tactical battles but losing the strategic war for global economic influence [E6][E17][E23].

Open Questions

Have Iran and Russia finalized any major military procurement contracts (Su-35, S-400) despite secondary sanctions? Satellite imagery and intelligence reports are needed.

What Would Change Our Mind

The analysis would be significantly altered by the emergence of reliable quantitative data on the three inconclusive hypotheses: (1) Monthly trade statistics from China Customs showing yuan-denominated share and trade volumes with Russia—if these show a 20% increase and 50% yuan share, Hypothesis H1 would be supported. (2) Indian oil import data from PPAC or vessel tracking—if Russian crude imports are up 30% and Saudi imports down 10%, Hypothesis H2 would be confirmed. (3) IMF COFER data showing a dollar share decline below 57% would validate H4. Additionally, declassified intelligence on Iran-Russia arms deals would resolve H3. Finally, a large-scale survey of foreign financial institutions on their compliance decisions with secondary sanctions would provide direct evidence on the mechanism of coercion. Any of these would substantially modify the confidence levels and policy recommendations.
