Institute position

International AI governance is achieving meaningful, if incomplete, convergence through a layered architecture of binding regulation (EU AI Act), soft-law instruments (G7 Hiroshima Process, OECD AI Principles), and ethical frameworks (UNESCO Recommendation), creating a de facto global baseline for trustworthy AI even as full legal harmonization remains elusive.

Confidence 62/100 · medium

Strongest objections

  • Full legal convergence on AI regulation is explicitly unlikely in the near term because different states emphasize fundamentally different policy goals (competitiveness vs. rights protection vs. industrial sovereignty), making the 'global baseline' narrative aspirational rather than descriptive.
  • The EU AI Act operates in a dynamic policy environment where competitiveness and industrial policy concerns actively tension rights protection, as demonstrated by the November 2025 delay of high-risk deadlines — suggesting the regulatory framework is already being eroded by political-economic pressures before full implementation.
  • The G7 framework explicitly acknowledges that 'like-minded approaches and policy instruments to achieve trustworthy AI may vary across G7 members despite shared common vision and goals,' undermining the claim of substantive convergence — the framework codifies disagreement as flexibility.

What would change our mind

  • If major adopters like Brazil, Japan, or Canada explicitly reject the EU risk-tiering model and pursue fundamentally different regulatory architectures.
  • If the EU AI Act's high-risk classifications are weakened significantly during implementation due to lobbying, reducing its global signaling power.
  • If empirical evidence shows that AI developers are avoiding the EU market due to regulatory costs, leading to a 'regulatory cold spot' effect.
