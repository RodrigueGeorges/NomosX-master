Existing policy frameworks — specifically the EU AI Act and secure-by-design guidelines — appear structurally insufficient to prevent adversarial attacks on LLMs in critical applications, particularly healthcare, based on the available evidence. The evidence suggests that prompt injection and retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) poisoning attacks achieve high manipulation rates with minimal effort in controlled settings E1E6, while healthcare organizations adopt AI faster than they update security practices, leaving patient data and clinical decisions potentially exposed E14. Effective defenses such as adversarial training and federated learning impose computational costs that smaller institutions may find prohibitive E10. Automated red-teaming, as demonstrated by GPT-Red, improves frontier model defenses but faces benchmark saturation and does not reach small-scale deployments E7. The alignment paradox further complicates mitigation: alignment techniques can increase vulnerability to injection attacks E1. It is important to note, however, that existing governance layers — including HIPAA security rules, FDA software validation requirements, and Europe’s Medical Device Regulation (MDR) — already impose certain robustness expectations for medical AI. These frameworks were not designed to address adversarial attacks specifically, and their coverage of LLM-specific vulnerabilities remains untested. Without binding requirements for adversarial robustness and resource support for underserved organizations, the vulnerability gap is likely to widen, increasing the probability of failures in healthcare AI systems. The analysis rests on several confirmed present facts, though with caveats. First, prompt injection and RAG poisoning are fundamental architectural vulnerabilities: five crafted documents achieve high manipulation in RAG systems, and visual prompt injection via mind maps attains a high attack success rate, threefold the baseline in controlled experiments E1E6. However, these findings come from academic studies that may not fully replicate in clinical environments. Second, healthcare cybersecurity is already lagging: ransomware attacks routinely deny patient care, new technologies outpace security updates, and smaller institutions lack compliance resources E14. Third, effective defenses like adversarial training and federated learning show superior resilience but increase computational overhead, making them potentially inaccessible to resource-constrained organizations E10. Fourth, the EU AI Act’s high-risk requirements impose compliance costs but do not address adversarial robustness, leaving a regulatory gap E4. Fifth, automated red-teaming (GPT-Red) has reduced prompt injection failures on GPT-5.6 Sol, but indirect injection benchmarks are saturated, suggesting diminishing returns E7. Finally, explainability methods can be weaponized to craft efficient adversarial attacks, turning transparency tools into attack vectors E11. These facts ground the causal chain linking architectural vulnerability, sector-specific resource constraints, and policy inadequacy, though direct evidence from real-world healthcare AI attacks remains limited [E11].

Strongest objections

  • Evolving policy frameworks can incorporate adversarial robustness requirements before catastrophic failures occur, especially given the precedent of the EU AI Act's risk-based approach [E7]. Existing layers like HIPAA, FDA premarket review, and EU MDR may already provide a foundation that can be adapted — though this has not been tested for LLM-specific attacks.
  • Technical advances in adversarial training and federated learning may reduce costs and become accessible to smaller institutions with open-source implementations.
  • The healthcare sector's existing cybersecurity regulations (e.g., HIPAA security rules, FDA software validation) may already provide a foundation that can be adapted to address AI-specific vulnerabilities, though their coverage of adversarial attacks remains unproven.
  • Human-in-the-loop oversight and clinical validation processes may catch adversarial manipulations before they cause patient harm, a factor the draft does not fully account for.

What would change our mind

  • A high-profile adversarial attack causing measurable patient harm in a regulated healthcare setting would force rapid policy action and validate the severity of the gap.
  • Evidence that advanced defenses (e.g., formal verification of LLMs against injection) are both effective and computationally affordable for small-to-medium healthcare providers.
  • A binding international standard for adversarial robustness in AI systems that gains widespread adoption and enforcement.
  • Empirical demonstration that existing regulatory frameworks (FDA, HIPAA, MDR) successfully block adversarial attacks on LLMs in clinical practice, reducing the vulnerability gap.
  • Longitudinal data showing a decline in adversarial attack success rates in healthcare deployments, suggesting current defenses are adequate.
